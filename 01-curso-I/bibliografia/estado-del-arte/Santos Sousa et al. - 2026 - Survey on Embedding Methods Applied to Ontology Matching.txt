Ontology matching plays a critical role in data integration, knowledge discovery, and ontology merging by establishing
correspondences between entities from diverse ontologies. Although traditional methods have proven efective in speciic
scenarios, they often struggle to capture the semantic depth and contextual nuances of ontology entities, especially when
applied to large, heterogeneous, and complex ontologies. Embedding-based methods have gained prominence in recent years.
Their efectiveness stems from their ability to encode semantic and contextual information into dense vector representations,
which enables more robust similarity computations. This survey provides a comprehensive review of embedding-based
techniques applied to ontology matching, with a focus on the schema-level of ontologies. A categorization framework is
proposed that identiies the key features of the diferent proposals and classiies them according to the embedding type, training
requirements, and context handling. In addition, this work highlights the challenges and opportunities in embedding-based
ontology matching.

1 Introduction
Ontology matching is the task of identifying correspondences (usually semantically equivalent entities ś classes,
properties, and instances) across diferent ontologies, encompassing both schema-level components (e.g., classes,
properties) and instance-level data (e.g., individuals, literals). It is a task recognized as crucial in a range of real
applications, such as data integration, knowledge fusion, semantic search, and automated reasoning. Despite the
ield’s maturity, traditional schema-level approaches ś based on lexical, structural, or logic-based heuristics ś
still struggle with challenges such as heterogeneous vocabularies, diverse modeling conventions, and contextdependent semantics. These limitations have motivated a growing interest in embedding techniques embeddings,
which ofer a data-driven way to capture semantic similarity. In the context of this work, embedding is a learned
vector representation of an ontology’s entities (such as classes, properties, and individuals). Based on the principles
of distributional semantics [31] (that is, the idea that entities occurring in similar contexts tend to have similar
meanings), these representations enable semantic comparisons that go beyond surface-level string or structural
similarity.

Consequently, embeddings ofer a powerful alternative for ontology matching. Unlike traditional lexical
techniques, embeddings map entities into a continuous vector space where geometric proximity relects semantic
similarity. This allows matchers to capture subtle semantic relationships, such as synonymy, hyponymy, and
homonymy, which surface-level string comparisons often miss. Furthermore, these representations can handle

Despite rapid advances in applying embeddings to ontology matching, the ield lacks a uniied and comprehensive synthesis. While existing surveys address narrow subtopics such as the use of external knowledge sources
[91], deep learning techniques, [40] the encoding of ontology entities with embeddings [21], and entity alignment
[30, 142]; they do not focus on constructing the embeddings speciically for ontology matching tasks. There
remains a gap in understanding how embedding methods are used, what kinds of information they encode,
and how efective they are in various matching scenarios, particularly complex ones. This survey addresses the
following key research questions: RQ1: How are embeddings used to represent ontological entities for matching?
RQ2: What types of embedding strategies have been applied to ontology matching, and how do they difer? RQ3:
How do existing methods handle the complexity and heterogeneity of real-world ontologies? RQ4: What are the
limitations of current embedding-based approaches, and what directions should future research take? To answer
these questions, this paper makes the following contributions:
• Comprehensive Survey: provide an up-to-date and systematic review of embedding approaches applied to
ontology matching at the schema-level, with a particular focus on embedding-based methods.
• Novel Classiication Framework: introduce a new classiication model for embedding-based ontology matching
approaches, based on three key dimensions: Lexical unit segmentation, Training strategy, and Complexity of
information representation (simple vs. complex matching).
• Gap Analysis and Comparison: analyze the strengths and limitations of each matcher, highlighting underexplored areas such as the use of embeddings in complex matching tasks.
• Extension of Prior Work: This survey expands on the work of Sousa et al. [105] by incorporating 81 recent
studies and introducing an additional dimension (simple vs. complex matching) to capture task complexity. It
relects the degree of expressiveness supported by embedding-based approaches in the correspondences they
generate.
• Future Research Directions: provide a forward-looking discussion on the role of embeddings in ontology
matching, including how they can complement external knowledge sources and improve alignment quality in
complex scenarios.

2.2

Embeddings for Ontologies

An essential step in matching approaches is measuring the similarity between ontology entities. Most approaches
rely on a lexical step that explores the terminological layer of ontologies (e.g., labels, comments, naming conventions). However, the limited representational capacity of lexical similarity metrics has motivated the integration
of alternative techniques. One such technique is the use of embeddings, multidimensional numerical vectors that
capture the semantics of various data types, including text, images, symbolic knowledge, and graph structures
[141]. These representations can be generated by machine learning models trained on large textual corpora or
derived from the structural features of symbolic systems, such as ontologies and knowledge graphs (KGs) [5].
Embeddings enable ontology matching by projecting entities from heterogeneous sources (lexical or structural)
into a shared vector space, where semantic similarity can be assessed through geometric proximity, such as
the angle between vectors [105]. This representation captures subtle semantic relations, including synonymy,
hyponymy, and homonyms, that traditional lexical techniques often overlook [95]. For instance, the embedding
vectors for the classes "Paper" and "Contribution" from diferent ontologies may exhibit high similarity even in
the absence of lexical overlap, facilitating the detection of conceptually related but non-identical correspondences,
as illustrated in Figure 3. Moreover, embeddings can address polysemy by learning multiple sense-speciic
representations and selecting the appropriate one based on contextual cues [9],. This capability helps disambiguate
entity meanings, thereby improving the robustness and accuracy of ontology matching systems.
The generic embedding-based matchers can be seen as a pipeline of irst encoding the ontology elements into
embedding vectors and then using these vectors to compare the similarity between entities, as illustrated in
Figure 4. The similarity between two entity embeddings is typically measured using the cosine similarity metric,
and if this similarity exceeds a predeined threshold, the entities are added to the inal alignment set. An ontology

Step 1: Embedding Generation. This initial stage involves creating vector representations for the entities

Step 2: Mapping Discovery. The generated embeddings are then employed to identify potential correspondences.
The literature describes many approaches: (i) directly calculating the distance or similarity between the embedding
vectors of two entities; (ii) training a supervised classiication model that uses the entity embeddings as input
features to predict a mapping; and (iii) jointly learning task-speciic embeddings with a matching model for
optimized performance.
2.2.1 Text Based. Generating ontology embeddings requires accounting for both structural properties and textual
metadata. This textual information, primarily entity labels and comments, provides critical natural language
descriptions. To encode these elements, common techniques leverage pre-trained word vectors and language
models such as Word2Vec [80], GloVe [88], fastText [13], and BERT [24], which ofer robust representations
derived from large-scale corpora.
Unlike static vectors (Word2Vec, GloVe, and fastText) that assign a single, ixed embedding to each vocabulary
term, contextual models such as ELMo [89], BERT, and SentenceBERT [96] generate dynamic representations.
While static models fail to distinguish polysemous words, assigning the same vector to "bank" regardless of

meaning, transformer-based architectures leverage multiple neural layers to produce distinct embeddings based
on surrounding tokens. This contextual sensitivity is critical when incorporating hierarchical or relational
metadata into entity embeddings. For multi-word sequences, representations are typically condensed into a single
vector using aggregation strategies such as mean pooling, the [CLS] token in BERT, or the inal hidden state in
LSTM networks.
An additional distinction among these language models lies in their tokenization process. In Word2Vec, each
word corresponds to a single embedding; for example, "Computer" and "Computers" are treated as distinct tokens
with their unique embeddings. Diferently, models like BERT use subword tokenization with methods such as
WordPiece. In this scheme, "Computer" might be split into pieces like "Comp" and "uter", while "Computers"
could be segmented into "Comp", "uter", and "s", with each piece having a corresponding embedding. The primary
advantage of this subword approach is its ability to handle out-of-vocabulary terms. While Word2Vec cannot
generate a representation for a word not present in its vocabulary, WordPiece can construct one by combining the
embeddings of its known subword units. This enhances the generalization of matching systems across diferent
domains without the need for retraining.
2.2.2 Graph Based. Along with textual information, in ontology matching, structural embeddings are desired
since they can encode more information into entity embeddings to improve matching performance and can deal
with the homonym problem to reject false positive correspondences. Diverse techniques for encoding graph
structures exist. One of the irst approaches is RDF2Vec [97]. In RDF2Vec, the ontology graph is traversed by
random walks, generating a document of sentences that are later used by an embedding model to generate
the entity embeddings. The original implementation of RDF2Vec splits the sentences in each entity and the
predicates connecting them, and uses Word2Vec to generate the inal embeddings. OWL2Vec [19] was designed
to address the challenges of embedding ontologies written in the OWL language, which provides constructs
such as intersectionOf and unionOf for representing complex entities. In OWL2Vec, a set of conversion rules
to translate OWL constructs into RDF is used. A similar random walk strategy is applied; however, multiple
documents are generated containing lexical, structural, and merged information. This method also generates
embeddings for individual entities as well as individual words. Since no labeled data is needed to train these
models, they can learn embeddings in an unsupervised way.
Another approach for learning embeddings from graph structures involves translational embeddings [55].
employed in models such as TransE [14], ConvE [23], or RotatE [110]. These methods associate each class and
property in an ontology with a randomly initialized embedding vector. The training process is iterative: for a

4 A Classification Framework for Embedding-Based Ontology Matching
This survey classiies matching systems based on their embedding-based similarity computation, terminological
feature handling, and training procedures. Building upon the framework in [105], we extend the "Lexical Unit
Segmentation" category to include subword segmentation and introduce a new "Alignment Type" category to
distinguish between simple and complex matching. As shown in Figure 6, our framework organizes systems
along four non-mutually exclusive dimensions: Lexical Unit Segmentation, Training, Information Level, and
Alignment Type. This categorization describes the information aggregated during entity embedding generation.
Because these categories overlap, a single model may occupy multiple classiications. For instance, a system
using word-level segmentation while incorporating external data like Wikipedia would be classiied under both
Word-level segmentation and Background Knowledge information levels.

4.1

Lexical Unit Segmentation

This category covers the various levels at which terminological data is segmented in matching systems. The
chosen segmentation level signiicantly impacts a matcher’s generalization capabilities and its performance on
the out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem [51]. This classiication framework extends the segmentation proposed in
[125] by adding the entity level, and is composed of four sub-categories (Figure 6), which are explained in the
subsequent sections. An example is given in Figure 7.
Entity-level segmentation generates a unique representation for each entity by treating its textual label as an
indivisible unit. Alternatively, a unique embedding can be assigned directly to an entity’s identiier, bypassing
textual content entirely, a method exempliied by RDF2Vec [97]. In this context, an entity’s entire terminological
label constitutes a single "sentence" or atomic token. For example, phrases like "accepted paper" and "reviewed
book" would each be assigned a single, unique embedding. A primary limitation of this approach is its inability
to represent out-of-vocabulary entities; a model cannot generate an embedding for "accepted book" if that
speciic sequence was absent during training, regardless of whether "accepted" and "book" were seen individually.
However, this method is computationally eicient, as the absence of aggregation or composition functions allows
for smaller, less complex processing models.

Entity-level segmentation generates a unique representation for each entity by treating its textual label as an

to represent out-of-vocabulary entities; a model cannot generate an embedding for "accepted book" if that
speciic sequence was absent during training, regardless of whether "accepted" and "book" were seen individually.
However, this method is computationally eicient, as the absence of aggregation or composition functions allows
for smaller, less complex processing models.
Word-level segmentation generates a distinct representation for each word within an entity’s terminological
layer. The inal entity representation is then computed by applying an aggregation function (e.g., sum or average)
to combine these individual word embeddings into a single vector. This is the standard approach adopted by
classic word embedding models such as GloVe [88].
For example, under a word-level segmentation scheme, the entity "accepted paper" is tokenized into its
constituent words, "accepted" and "paper." After retrieving the individual embedding for each word, an aggregation
function is applied to compute a single vector representation for the complete entity. Another aggregation strategy
can use the hidden state of a neural network; for instance, the inal output of a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
network can serve as the composite embedding. This type of segmentation ofers better generalization because
the model can construct a meaningful representation for an unseen entity, such as "accepted book," as long as the
embeddings for its constituent words ("accepted" and "book") are known.
Subword-level segmentation divides text into units smaller than a whole word, such as word fragments or
n-grams. This technique is a standard practice in many modern language models. For example, the WordPiece
tokenizer, used by BERT, might split the word "accepted" into "accep" and "ted." The vocabulary for such tokenizers
is learned from a large text corpus in a preliminary training step. Other common subword segmentation schemes
include Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) [74] and n-gram-based methods.
Character-level segmentation, the most granular approach, operates on individual characters within the
terminological layer. Its primary advantage is universality; by utilizing a character-based alphabet, it eliminates
the out-of-vocabulary problem and maintains a vocabulary orders of magnitude smaller than other methods.
However, this granularity requires models to learn meaningful representations from scratch. Consequently,
capturing semantic depth typically requires more complex architectures, such as additional neural layers or larger
contextual windows, to compose word-level meaning from character sequences.

Training Paradigm

This section categorizes matching systems by their training procedures, relecting their dependency on speciic
ontology data and their subsequent generalization capabilities. The three categories are: (i) Pre-trained: Systems
trained exclusively on corpora external to the input ontologies; (ii) Trained: Systems enploy the input ontologies

to either train a model from scratch or ine-tune a pre-trained one; (iii) Reference: A subset of the "Trained"
category, speciically requiring supervised training on reference alignments.
The Pre-train category encompasses systems that employ models trained on external data before the matching
process begins. This includes systems that leverage general-purpose pre-trained models, such as BERT, or standard
word embeddings. The pre-training itself is often performed on tasks unrelated to ontology matching, with
common objectives like masked language modeling or data reconstruction.
The Trained category includes systems that require training or ine-tuning using data from the ontologies
being aligned, resulting in representations tailored for each matching task. When a system uses a pre-trained
model, this process is equivalent to ine-tuning. For example, a system might reine its word embeddings using
synonyms extracted directly from the input ontologies. Systems may also be trained on a speciic task prior to
pre-training on another.
The Reference category consists of supervised systems that require an annotated dataset of reference alignments to train their models. This process adjusts the internal parameters of the embedding-based similarity
models, such as network weights. These reference alignments, which function as the ground truth, can be created
manually by domain experts, generated automatically, or produced by a "teacher" matching system.

4.3

Information Level

This section details the Information Level category, which classiies systems based on the scope of information
they aggregate to generate entity embeddings. It distinguishes between four levels: No Context, Context, Relation,
and Background Knowledge. Note that these levels are not entirely independent. Incorporating Background
Knowledge from external sources is an approach that can be combined with any other information level. A
system that employs information from neighboring entities is classiied as Contextual. If it explicitly encodes the
type of properties to those entities, it is classiied under both the Context and Relation levels.
The No Context level includes systems that generate an embedding for an entity using exclusively the
information contained within that entity itself. Under this approach, each entity is treated as an isolated object,
and information from other entities or their relationships is disregarded. A matcher at this information level
generates an entity’s inal embedding by exclusively using its internal features, such as labels and comments,
while disregarding any of its relationships to other entities. For example, a non-contextual embedding for the
class Paper relies solely on its own internal features, whereas a contextual embedding integrates information

as a subClassOf property).
The Relation level includes systems that explicitly encode the type of properties an entity has with its
neighbors when generating embeddings [126]. Unlike at the Context level, a matcher here distinguishes between
diferent kinds of properties, such as inheritance and disjointness. This distinction is important because diferent
properties imply diferent similarity assumptions. For example, entities in a disjoint property should have lower
similarity scores than entities in a parent-child hierarchy. Integrating such relational information guides the
model towards embeddings that more accurately relect these semantic constraints.
The Background Knowledge level comprises systems that enrich entity embeddings by incorporating
information from sources external to the input ontologies, such as Wikipedia or WordNet [91]. This approach
allows a matcher to inject supplementary information beyond what is explicitly stated in the ontology. For
ACM Comput. Surv.

Survey on Embedding Methods Applied to Ontology Matching • 11

instance, if an entity lacks a descriptive comment, an external source can provide this missing text, which is
then aggregated to generate a more comprehensive embedding. The key distinction from the Context level is the
origin of the information: the Context level uses only data already internal to the input ontologies, whereas the
Background Knowledge level leverages external data sources, including Wikipedia, Thesaurus, Vocabulary, etc.

4.4

Alignment Cardinality and Complexity

This section categorizes systems based on the alignment complexity they address, distinguishing between
simple and complex matching tasks. This classiication is important for understanding how the capabilities of
diferent systems vary with the intricacy of the required correspondences. Simple alignment systems operate on
atomic entities and are designed to produce one-to-one (1:1) correspondences. In this model, each entity from a
source ontology is matched to a single, unique entity in the target ontology. In contrast, Complex alignment
systems handle intricate correspondences that involve complex entities, in other words, those deined by logical
combinations of other entities (e.g., using unions, intersections, or restrictions). These systems can produce
many-to-many (n:m) alignments and are often necessary for handling constructs such as blank nodes in OWL
ontologies.

Approaches Using No Contextual Information

This section reviews systems classiied under the No Context category, grouping them by methodological
similarity. As deined in Section 4, these systems generate representations for an entity using only its own

Word2Vec-Based Representations. Zhang et al. [143] is recognized as the irst to apply embeddings to ontology

moving beyond purely lexical metrics. Speciically, techniques such as SVD and Word2Vec were combined with
lexical measures to compute similarity. A key limitation of their approach was the absence of a robust method for

Hybrid Embedding and Lexical Similarity. A prevalent approach in the literature involves constructing hybrid
systems that combine embedding techniques with traditional lexical similarity metrics. For example, the work
by Fallatah et al. [29] integrates the Levenshtein lexical similarity metric with word embeddings, selecting the
maximum value between the two scores to determine the inal similarity. Similarly, the approach in [36] irst
employs lexical metrics to compute an initial similarity matrix, which is subsequently combined with GloVe
embeddings trained on PubMed data to produce the inal alignment. The DOME matcher [46] also combines
lexical and embedding-based methods, but does so sequentially. An initial similarity score is computed using
lexical metrics and is then adjusted using Doc2Vec embeddings to produce the inal similarity scores. Focusing
on the speciic domain of property matching, LEAPME [10] employs a distinct hybrid technique that relies on
the embedding similarity between labels alongside a set of hand-engineered metrics.
Embedding Reinement and Neural Augmentation. Despite their lexibility, pre-trained embeddings have a
notable limitation: they are static, assigning a single, ixed representation to each term. This makes it diicult to
handle homonymous entities, whose meaning may vary depending on the context in which they appear. One
strategy to address this issue is supervised reinement, a process that contextualises pre-trained embeddings to
the speciic semantics of the ontologies under analysis. The works in [66, 67] reine a set of pre-trained vectors
by contrasting the representations of positively aligned entity pairs with those of negative ones. The DeepFCA
matcher [70] implements a related reinement strategy, employing formal concept analysis to generate a lattice
of related entities that are then approximated in the embedding space, while negative samples are extracted from
disjoint properties.
Another contextualization strategy involves using neural networks to further process or reine existing
embeddings. Xue et al. [135] employ Word2Vec embeddings as input features to a neural network with the aim
of improving the inal similarity computation. A similar approach is adopted in WETA [34], which combines the
cosine similarity derived from fastText embeddings with classiication scores produced by a neural network. The
study in [121] enriches a hand-engineered feature vector with the output of a neural network to compute similarity
between entities. In a complementary direction, neural networks can also be used to generate embeddings directly.
The ERSOM matcher [133], for example, uses an autoencoder to learn entity representations from a set of
input features. Because the autoencoder’s training objective is to reconstruct its input from a compressed latent
representation, it can be trained in an unsupervised manner, making it particularly suitable for tasks in which
reference alignments are unavailable.
Transformer-Based Approaches. With recent advances in NLP, powerful models based on the Transformer
architecture, such as BERT, have been widely adopted. BERT is often described as a "contextual" model because
it generates a unique representation for a word conditioned on its surrounding text. However, it is important
to distinguish this notion of contextuality from the deinition used in this survey. Here, entity context refers
speciically to information derived from an entity’s neighbours within the ontology structure, such as its parent and
child classes. Accordingly, systems that apply transformer-based architectures to an entity’s textual information
in isolation, without relying on this broader ontological context, are classiied as "No Context" systems. For
instance, the TOM and Fine-TOM matchers [65, 68] use transformer models to generate representations from
entity text, which are then compared using cosine similarity.
A prevalent strategy for transformer-based models is ine-tuning on task-speciic data. Fine-TOM, for instance,
is retrained using known entity correspondences, while the approach in [72] leverages formal concept analysis
to derive training data for a sentence transformer. Beyond ine-tuning, models are commonly adapted through
specialised downstream classiication heads. In ontology matching, a standard approach adds such a head to predict
the alignment probability between entity pairs, as demonstrated in [37, 44, 49]. Similarly, the approach in [43]
pairs a pre-trained BERT model with a binary classiier, further reining the process by averaging probabilities
for multi-label entities and employing inverted indexing to prune the candidate space. Other research explores
hybrid integrations: Kardos et al. [62] combine BERT embeddings with traditional lexical metrics, while Neutel et

Graph-Based Embeddings. RDF2Vec represents a foundational graph-based embedding approach in ontology
matching. It generates sentence-like sequences through random walks over the ontology graph and subsequently
processes these sequences with a Word2Vec model, treating each entity label as a single token to produce unique
embeddings. REMA [69] follows a similar pipeline, combining random walks and Word2Vec with cosine similarity
for alignment. Beyond direct similarity computation, these embeddings frequently serve as features for binary
classiication. For example, the system in [60] uses RDF2Vec embeddings to predict how changes in ontology
resources afect existing alignments, while AnyGraphMatcher [77] feeds various metrics, such as Euclidean
distance and skip-gram probability, into a classiier to predict entity correspondences. To mitigate the scarcity
of labelled data, AnyGraphMatcher automatically generates a gold-standard training set from initial similarity
scores, thereby enabling supervised learning without manual annotation.
When embeddings for two ontologies are generated independently, their respective vector spaces are often
unaligned, rendering standard similarity metrics unreliable. To address this problem, Portisch et al. [90] employ
linear transformation techniques to learn a rotation matrix that maps both sets of embeddings into a shared
vector space, after which entity similarity can be computed using standard metrics such as Euclidean distance.
Other works, such as those by Hertling et al. [48], learn a linear projection in a supervised manner to bring
the two embedding spaces into alignment. RDF2Vec has also been combined with other models to extend its
capabilities. For instance, DESKMatcher [82] evaluates several embedding models, (including Word2Vec, GloVe,
and RDF2Vec) and combines them with lexical similarity metrics. In another example, the work in [79] constructs
a hybrid representation by integrating BERT-based embeddings (derived from textual content) with RDF2Vecbased embeddings (generated from the ontology graph structure), evaluating the uniied embedding using cosine
similarity. RDF2Vec has further been applied to complex matching tasks; for instance, in [1, 2], an RDF2Vec-based
strategy is used to generate entity embeddings after a fuzziication method has been applied to the ontology.

iteratively adjusting embeddings to minimise the distance between a valid triple in the ontology, such as (�, �, �),

advantage of these techniques is their unsupervised nature: they do not require reference alignments to learn
entity representations. The work in [4] extends translational techniques by modelling entities in hyperbolic space,
enabling the embeddings to more efectively capture complex predicates such as inverses and compositional
patterns. A related distance-based learning technique is StarSpace [131], which operates on a similar contrastive
learning principle, training the model to pull embeddings of similar entities closer together while pushing
them apart from randomly selected negative samples, thereby ensuring a clearer separation between entities

Approaches Exploiting Contextual Information

This section reviews systems that exploit contextual information derived from neighboring entities to compute
entity similarity. Such contextual information typically originates from the class hierarchy (encompassing
parent and child classes) and associated properties or instances when available. A variety of approaches have
been proposed for aggregating this information, each following a distinct methodology. The section provides a
structured overview of these approaches, highlighting how diferent systems incorporate and process contextual
data to improve matching accuracy. The embedding models employed by each work are summarized in Table 3
Contextual Text Enrichment. A irst strategy consists of incorporating textual information from an entity’s
structural context directly into its textual description, thereby enriching its representation before encoding. For
example, [56] constructs a sentence that concatenates entity labels with those of two parent classes, which is
then encoded using character embeddings and aggregated through an LSTM network; the resulting embedding
is passed to a classiication head to predict correspondence probability. A similar approach is adopted in [120],
where hierarchy labels are incorporated into class entity labels, and domain and range information is appended
to property labels; the enriched text is subsequently processed by a sentence transformer, and entity similarity is
measured using cosine similarity. This approach additionally applies a reasoner prior to matching to infer new
relationships, and targets cross-lingual ontology matching scenarios. Truveta Mapper [6] follows a comparable
strategy by generating smart IDs for each entity that encode hierarchical informationÐconcretely, a child entity’s
ID contains the IDs of its ancestor concepts. These smart IDs are fed into the ByT5 model, operating at the character
level, to produce inal embeddings; the model is further ine-tuned using synonym information extracted from
the ontologies. TEXTO [87] similarly combines entity information with label descriptions to compute similarity
using language models, a strategy also employed in [119]. In the context of complex ontology matching, [107]
proposes an integrated approach that leverages LLMs to generate expressive correspondences. Extending the
CANARD framework, the method focuses on matching semantically similar surroundings of instance sub-graphs,
guided by user knowledge needs expressed as SPARQL queries. Four architectural modiications are introduced:
label embedding similarity (LES), embeddings of SPARQL queries (ESQ), subgraph embeddings (SE), and instance
embeddings (IE). Levenshtein similarity is replaced by embedding-based similarity in key pipeline steps, using
LLMs to encode textual information for more expressive similarity assessment. Experiments on the OAEI
Conference benchmark demonstrate that integrating LLMs signiicantly enhances performanceÐparticularly in
the LES and ESQ conigurations, outperforming baseline and state-of-the-art systems in precision and F-measure
without requiring reference alignments or ine-tuning.

classiier ensemble; some metrics are derived from embeddings provided by the SpaCy library [50], and a blocking
strategy is applied to reduce the number of candidate pairs and improve runtime eiciency. Another approach
combining embeddings with classiiers is introduced in [103], which proposes a self-matching training method
for Inverted-Index Matrix Embedding (InME) and Co-occurrence Matrix Embedding (CoME) models to improve

locations), while CoME captures local information (word co-occurrences within axioms). Self-matching training
enhances a binary classiier’s ability to recognize subclassśsuperclass relationships, improving robustness for
isolated entities. Evaluations on the GO, FoodOn, and HeLiS ontologies show that InME with self-matching
training outperforms existing ontology embeddings, and that concatenating CoME with OWL2Vec* yields
superior performance on the HeLiS ontology. In BERT-INT [54], transformer-generated similarities are combined
with structural information via a similarity matrix. PropMatch [106], which focuses on property matching, uses
language models to generate embeddings for property-related entities and aggregates the resulting similarities to
determine property correspondences.
GNN-Based Approaches. GNNs constitute a particularly expressive class of models for aggregating contextual
information in ontology matching. GNNs rely on iterative aggregation steps that combine entity embeddings
with those of their neighbors, propagating information through multiple layers to capture context at varying
depths. Several GNN variants have been employed in this setting, including message-passing networks, Graph
Convolutional Networks (GCNs), and graph attention networks (GATs). Initial entity embeddings can either
be learned through a dedicated embedding layer or generated by a language model from the entity’s textual
description. VeeAlign [52, 53] is a supervised model that initializes entity embeddings using the Universal
Sentence Encoder (USE) to encode textual descriptions. Context from neighboring entities is organized into
four facetsÐancestral entities, child entities, and entities connected via object and datatype properties, each
treated as a path; node-level attention aggregates entities within each path, and path-level attention generates a

18

• G. S. Sousa et al.

uniied context embedding. This context embedding is concatenated with the initial entity embedding and passed
through a Siamese network to produce the inal representation, with cosine similarity used for cross-ontology
comparison. Inspired by VeeAlign, GraphMatcher [27] replaces the facet-based aggregation with a GAT, using
USE-encoded embeddings as input. Wu et al. [130] similarly employ a GAT but compute initial embeddings
using Siamese transformer encoders; inal embeddings are compared using cosine similarity, and an automatic
threshold estimation module based on Greedy Matching and Stable Marriage algorithms is incorporated. DAEOM
[129] adopts an analogous architecture but replaces Siamese transformer encoders with BERT coupled with
a pooling layer. In the NLFOA framework [102], the graph structure of ontology entities is converted into a
textual representation that encodes hierarchical relationships and connecting properties; this text is then used to
generate embeddings for similarity comparison. MultiEA [139] addresses the problem of simultaneously aligning
multiple (more than two) KGs in a single pass. The proposed framework embeds entities from all candidate KGs
into a shared feature space using a common GNN-based encoder and explores three alignment strategiesÐmean,
anchor, and pairwiseÐto minimize distances among pre-aligned entities. An inference enhancement technique
that incorporates high-order similarities is also introduced. Two new real-world benchmark datasets (DBP-4 and
DWY-3) and a novel evaluation metric (M-Hits@K) are proposed to validate the framework. OM-GGAT [22]
combines semantic and structural embedding representations using a gated graph attention model. Semantic
knowledge and path information are encoded using OWL2Vec, and ontologies are transformed into graph
structures; a GAT then assigns attention weights to neighboring nodes for structural feature extraction, and
gated networks fuse the semantic and structural embeddings. Experiments on public datasets demonstrate the
efectiveness of this approach in improving matching eiciency.
While GNN-based architectures are capable of modeling rich contextual lows, they are typically supervised,
requiring reference alignments to train model weights, a signiicant challenge given the scarcity of labeled
ontology matching datasets. To address this limitation, OntoConnect [15] adopts an unsupervised approach
that leverages GNNs for embedding generation. A related study [16, 17] uses fastText to generate initial entity
embeddings, which are subsequently contextualized through a GNN and trained with a negative sampling strategy.
Another variant [15] employs fastText for initial embeddings and an LSTM-based recursive graph network to
compute embeddings across diferent hierarchical levels. Embeddings are averaged and propagated to higher
network layers, and the model is trained via a contextual entity prediction objective, enabling cosine similarity
computation between source and target embeddings after training. CLOM [11] is an unsupervised cross-lingual
ontology matching method that fuses structural and semantic similarity. It addresses the twin limitations of
supervised approaches (which sufer from a lack of training data) and current unsupervised approaches that rely
solely on lexical similarity. CLOM uses LaBSE, a state-of-the-art multilingual text embedding model, to embed
entity descriptions into low-dimensional vectors for semantic similarity computation. These semantic similarities
are used to generate initial seed alignments, which in turn inform the learning of structural embeddings (e.g.,
node2vec, GCN, RGCN, TransE) over a joint graph; a weighted combination of semantic and structural similarities
determines the inal alignment. Experiments on the MultiFarm dataset across GermanśEnglish, GermanśFrench,
and EnglishśFrench language pairs demonstrate substantial improvements over lexical methods, conirming the
complementarity of structural and semantic embeddings for cross-lingual matching.
LLM-Based Contextual Matching. LLMs have emerged as powerful context-aware encoders and reasoning
modules for ontology matching. LLMs4OM [35] evaluates the efectiveness of LLMs in ontology matching
through a dual-module strategy: a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) module for candidate selection and an
LLM-based matching module for ine-grained alignment. Zero-shot prompting is explored across three ontology
representationsÐclass, class-parent, and class-children. Extensive evaluations on 20 ontology matching datasets
show that LLMs within LLMs4OM can match or even surpass traditional matching systems, particularly in complex
scenarios. The unsupervised, domain-agnostic HybridOM [118] combines vector and string-based similarity
through hybrid search. Entity representations are enriched by using LLMs to generate additional descriptions,

integrating both ontology knowledge and LLM-generated content. The pipeline involves data preprocessing
(extracting raw data, verbalizing axioms with DeepOnto, and generating biomedical descriptions with LLaMA3),
embedding generation (using gtr-t5-large and BioLord-2023), and ingestion into an OpenSearch vector database;
the core matching process combines k-NN and BM25 retrieval with a dual low-directional search. Evaluations
on the Bio-ML 2024 track demonstrate high F1-score and recall, particularly in complex cases. MILA [111]
embeds a retrieve-identify-prompt pipeline within a prioritized depth-irst search (PDFS) strategy, addressing
both the limited performance of LLM-based systems on complex scenarios and their high computational overhead.
MILA irst identiies high-conidence bidirectional (HCB) correspondences using fast heuristics, reserving LLM
queries for borderline cases; candidate retrieval leverages SBERT embeddings for semantic similarity. Evaluated
on biomedical, anatomy, and biodiversity tasks from OAEI 2024, MILA achieves the highest F-measure in ive

independent planning modules and tools but a shared memory. The Retrieval Agent extracts entity information
(metadata, syntactic, lexical, and semantic) and stores it in a hybrid relational-and-vector database.The Matching

rank fusion, validates them through LLM-driven self-checking, and merges bidirectional results. This design
mitigates both retrieval cost and LLM hallucination. Evaluations on the OAEI Conference, Anatomy, and MSE
tracks show competitive performance on simple matching tasks and signiicant improvements on complex and
few-shot tasks.

5.4

Approaches Leveraging Background Knowledge

Ontologies frequently lack exhaustive information about all their entities; some entities may have only a label
and no accompanying deinition or comment. In such cases, the available information may be insuicient for
constructing adequately representative embeddings. To address this limitation, several matchers inject background
knowledge from external sources into the embedding generation process. The authors in [122] use Word2Vec
embeddings to encode entity textual information, which is subsequently aggregated through an LSTM. Four
views are employed (names, synonyms, deinitions, and Wikipedia context) to construct entity embeddings that
are passed to a neural classiier for correspondence prediction. Similarly, Wang [123] relies on Wikipedia as
background knowledge through three complementary views: a semantic view generated via an LSTM, a structural
view aggregated using a graph attention network, and a deinition view embedded using Sentence-BERT; the
combined representation is then used to compute cosine similarity. Li et al. [73] adopt a further multi-view
strategy, composing inal embeddings from lexical, structural, and resource views, where the resource view
links source and target entities to an external reference ontology. Satti et al. [99] use entity names and types
as the basis for embedding composition, querying UMLS with suixes extracted from these texts to retrieve
supplementary information, which is subsequently encoded by a Transformer model. In OTMapOnto [7], fastText
and BioWordVec embeddings are averaged to produce entity representations, enriched with synset information
from WordNet, and compared using the Wasserstein distance. The ALOD2Vec matcher [92] queries an external
RDF2Vec-based vector dataset using entity lexical information and computes inal similarity via cosine similarity.

6 Open Challenges and Research Gaps
6.1 Trends in Embedding-Based Ontology Matching
The analysis conducted in this survey reveals several challenges requiring further attention.
Generalization problem. A recurrent limitation associated with entity-level segmentation in ontology matching
is its restricted capacity to generalize across domains. The absence of a shared vocabulary needs retraining the
model for each previously unseen ontology, thereby hindering the cumulative acquisition of knowledge across
matching tasks. For example, entity embeddings learned in the biomedical domain are unlikely to contain the
same terminology as those required in the conference domain. Furthermore, even in cases where an entity label
ACM Comput. Surv.

Survey on Embedding Methods Applied to Ontology Matching • 23

appears identically across domains, its semantic interpretation may difer substantially from that encoded during
training, giving rise to ambiguity. Without the capacity to exploit patterns in textual information, generalizing a
matcher remains challenging even within a single domain. As the number of unique embeddings to be learned
grows, sparsity issues emerge in the embedding space, complicating knowledge reuse. Additionally, without
analyzing the textual information for each entity, it becomes impossible to eliminate noisy information or to
leverage commonalities in knowledge, such as comments and descriptions.
Lack of shared vocabulary. Another issue that arises from this level of segmentation is the lack of shared
vocabulary, which prevents embeddings from diferent ontologies from relating to each other. Due to the random
initialization of neural models, the same entity can have diferent embeddings in two distinct ontologies when
learned through these methods, as they are encoded in separate embedding spaces. The work in [90] illustrates
this problem in embeddings generated by RDF2Vec using two identical copies of the same graph generated from
synthetic data. To address this, the authors propose learning a rotation matrix from anchor correspondences
to transform one embedding space into the other. They also demonstrate that introducing heterogeneity, by
randomly removing triples from one of the graphs, signiicantly reduces matching performance. A similar issue
was observed in LogMap-ML [20], in which OWL2Vec embeddings were used for matching. The independence
problem, caused by the generation of separate embedding spaces during independent OWL2Vec training, was
resolved by feeding the embeddings into a siamese network. It was trained using anchor correspondences to
learn a transformation function that aligns the two embedding spaces.
Structural divergence. Embedding methods that rely exclusively on ontology structure, without incorporating
the textual information associated with each entity, are inherently sensitive to structural diferences between the
ontologies being aligned. Consequently, such methods tend to exhibit reduced performance when applied to
ontology pairs with substantially divergent structural organizations. This limitation is evidenced in Hertling et
al. [? ], where RDF2Vec is evaluated across ontologies from diferent domains, yielding strong results when the
compared ontologies share similar structural patterns but markedly lower performance when their structures
difer. Although RDF2Vec efectively encodes the structural relationships among entities within an ontology graph,
structural proximity does not necessarily entail semantic equivalence - the primary objective of alignment. For
instance, entities such as "Author" and "Book" may co-occur frequently within the same ontological neighbourhood
due to their relational proximity, causing their embeddings to be placed in close proximity in the vector space.
Despite exhibiting high cosine similarity, these entities are semantically distinct, and a matcher relying solely on
such structural embeddings risks producing erroneous correspondences between them.
Need for training alignments. A signiicant portion of the reviewed systems (48%) relies on reference alignments
for training. These alignments are typically generated by domain experts or automatic systems and establish
equivalence relations between entities across ontologies. As no large alignment dataset is currently available,
many systems resort to the reference alignments provided by the OAEI tracks for model training. However, since
these alignments are also used for evaluation, systems trained on such data may sufer from generalisation issues.
Alternative approaches treat other matching systems as teachers, using their alignment outputs as training data,
or adopt alignment hypotheses such as exact label matching as a gold standard. These strategies assume that
embedding models can extrapolate knowledge and identify additional correspondences, thereby increasing the
number of available alignments. However, when such systems are also evaluated in competitive settings, alignment
biases may arise, further resulting in generalization problems. To address these limitations, unsupervised training
methods have been proposed, embedding ontologies into a shared vector space and inferring alignments from
entity similarity. However, these approaches remain under development and require further investigation to
assess their efectiveness.
Coverage of properties. Few works explore the integration of property-speciic information into the embedding
generation process for similarity computation. Certain properties, such as disjointness, indicate a negative efect
on similarity, and merely including the object values they represent can lead to misleading results, causing
ACM Comput. Surv.

24

• G. S. Sousa et al.

disjoint entities to appear more similar than they are. Integrating property-speciic information into embedding
generation can therefore enhance the accuracy of alignment systems. For instance, incorporating domain and
range information into property embeddings, or explicitly encoding disjointness and other constraints, can
provide a richer context for entity representations.
Background knowledge. Few systems investigate the integration of background knowledge as supplementary
information for generating embeddings. Integrating such external knowledge can ofer additional context and
information for entities that lack suicient resources. For example, leveraging external knowledge sources like
Wikipedia or WordNet can enrich entities with textual descriptions or semantic annotations, improving the
quality of the generated embeddings. However, this integration is not without drawbacks, as the incorporation of
external data may introduce noise into the embedding space and increase the computational overhead of the
alignment process. Consequently, further research is needed to examine the trade-ofs between accuracy and
eiciency in matching systems that adopt such strategies based on the integration of external information during
embedding generation.
Complex matching. Despite the importance of complex correspondences in real-world ontology alignment,
only a limited number of approaches have explored the application of embeddings to complex matching tasks.
In such tasks, the aggregation of multiple constituent entities into a uniied representation of a complex entity
introduces additional diiculty in deining the boundaries and scope of meaningful contextual information.
Speciically, a matching system must determine the extent to which the contextual information associated with
each constituent entity contributes to the overall context of the composed complex entity. When complex entities
and their potential contexts are modeled as subgraphs, the number of candidate combinations to be considered
grows exponentially, resulting in worst-case time complexity that poses a signiicant scalability barrier in complex
matching scenarios.

6.2

Answering the Research uestions

This survey presents a systematic analysis of 103 embedding-based ontology matching approaches, providing
evidence-based answers to the research questions formulated in Section 1. In the following, the each research
question is addressed in turn, synthesizing the key indings across the four proposed classiication dimensions
(lexical segmentation, training paradigm, information scope, and alignment complexity) with reference to the
surveyed literature.
RQ1: How are embeddings used to represent ontological entities for matching? Embeddings are one of the main
semantic similarity measures of current matching systems, and their representational capacity varies based on
three critical design choices revealed through this survey analysis:
• the choice of lexical segmentation strategy has a strong impact on the generalization capability of the
embeddings in a matcher: the survey shows 46% of approaches employ subword segmentation (primarily
via transformer-family models), 35% use word-level segmentation, 18% adopt entity-level representations
(e.g., RDF2Vec), and only 6% utilize character-level approaches. Entity-level embeddings face generalization
limitations, as demonstrated in [90], because most of the techniques used in creating those embeddings often
produce them independently for source and target ontologies that occupy diferent vector spaces, requiring
alignment techniques like rotation matrices to make them relect the similarity measure required.
• the information level determines the amount of information considered for similarity computation: half
of surveyed systems generate embeddings considering only entity-related features (labels, comments), while
the other half incorporate contextual information from neighboring entities. Crucially, only 12% explicitly
encode relational semantics (e.g., distinguishing subClassOf from disjointWith). Not being able to encode
properties in the embeddings can cause erroneous matches (e.g., placing semantically disjoint entities close in
ACM Comput. Surv.

Survey on Embedding Methods Applied to Ontology Matching • 25

embedding space). Then, background knowledge integration remains underutilized (9% of systems), though
works like [123] demonstrate its value for entities lacking descriptive text.
• considering training-related properties and the types of correspondences produced by matching systems,
a notable pattern emerges: while 70% of systems employ pre-trained embedding models, 68% additionally inetune these models on ontology-speciic data in order to adapt representations to domain semantics. This trend
suggests that general-purpose pre-trained models are insuicient for capturing domain-speciic similarities
in isolation, necessitating some degree of task-speciic adaptation. Additionally, 38% of the reviewed works
require reference alignments during training. The reliance on such alignments introduces a practical limitation,
as curated alignment data is scarce.
RQ2: What types of embedding strategies have been applied to ontology matching, and how do they difer? The
analysis in this survey reveals the usage of multiple types of embedding strategies, but most of them follow some
trends. The dominant paradigm has evolved from static word embeddings (2015ś2018) toward transformer-based
contextual embeddings (2019śpresent), but with critical variations:
• Lexical/Semantic Embeddings: These approaches use word embeddings (e.g., Word2Vec, GloVe) or contextual
embeddings (e.g., BERT) to capture semantic information from entity labels and textual descriptions. They
emphasize language understanding but often overlook ontology structure.
• Graph-Based Embeddings: These methods represent ontologies as graphs and use algorithms like RDF2Vec,
TransE, or GCNs to learn embeddings that encode structural information. They can better capture hierarchical
and relational patterns, but may struggle with sparse or inconsistent graphs.
• Hybrid Approaches: Combining lexical and structural features, hybrid models aim to leverage the strengths
of both. Some methods concatenate multiple embeddings, while others use attention or fusion mechanisms.
These tend to perform better in varied scenarios but may be more complex and computationally demanding.
The primary diferences lie in the types of input data they use (text vs. structure), the embedding architectures
(e.g., static vs. dynamic), and the extent to which they generalize across domains.
RQ3: How do existing methods handle the complexity and heterogeneity of real-world ontologies? Current methods
address heterogeneity through four evidence-based strategies, but with signiicant limitations revealed in this
work:
• Multi-modal embeddings shows promise but remains under-engineered: while most works combine lexical
and structural features, only 9% incorporate background knowledge. Also, the mechanisms of embedding
combination are often simplistic (e.g., averaging similarities), which can introduce noise in the similarity computation. Some works demonstrate that property-aware graph networks can better handle medical ontologies’
deep hierarchies, but such integration is diicult to implement, and not all works explored them.
• Context-aware adaptation addresses lexical heterogeneity efectively: approaches incorporating hierarchical
context (e.g., parent/child labels) can achieve higher F-measure since aggregating this information can improve
the similarity computation used in the matching process however, as can be seen in Table 6 from the total
distribution of all classiication combinations of embedding used in the reviewed works, the relation aware
embeddings appears in only 0.05% in total. This reveals that most context aggregation methods treat all
neighbors equally, ignoring that disjointWith relations, as described in RQ1 discussion, should decrease
similarity while subClassOf should increase it.
• Focused on simple correspondences Most of the current approaches focuses in inding only simple correspondences. Only 7% of surveyed works propose approaches that can ind complex correspondences. Most of
the real-world ontologies require those types of correspondences to be aligned. Since only a few works propose
applications of embeddings in such cases, this demonstrates that the proposed approaches still have diiculties
in dealing with real-world ontology heterogeneities.

Along with those considerations, only one work explored the generation of embeddings aggregating both
relational and background knowledge in the information level category. This analysis demonstrates that most of

simultaneously.
RQ4: What are the limitations of current embedding-based approaches, and what directions should future research
take? This synthesis identiies four interconnected limitations that form a research agenda grounded in empirical
evidence:
• The supervision bottleneck: 38% of approaches require reference alignments for training, creating a circular
dependency where matching quality depends on existing alignments. This severely limits applicability to
novel domains. Unsupervised approaches exist, but underperform supervised ones. Future work must develop
weakly supervised paradigms that leverage minimal human input (e.g., fewer seed alignments) combined with
self-training on promising directions that also extend to complex matching scenarios.
• The structural semantics gap: Current embeddings poorly represent logical constraints (e.g., disjointness,
cardinality restrictions, intersections). This analysis shows only 7% of systems handle complex correspondences
involving logical constructorsÐdespite most of the real-world ontology alignments requiring such expressions
[116]. Future embeddings must move beyond node/edge representations to encode complex entities (constructed
with logical predicates such as union or intersection), potentially through neuro-symbolic integration or
geometric embeddings in constraint-satisfying spaces (e.g., hyperbolic manifolds for hierarchy preservation),
also dealing with the embedding shared vector space problem.
• The explainability deicit: Embedding-based matches provide similarity scores without justiication, hindering expert validation. While the current approaches are searching for improved f-measure scores, incorporating
explainability mechanisms is still in the second plan. Given that ontology matching can occur in high-stakes
domains (healthcare, inance), future work must develop interpretable similarity metrics that surface evidence for
matches. One possible direction is through attention visualization over ontology subgraphs or counterfactual
explanations.
• The scalability-complexity trade-of: Hybrid approaches achieve the highest accuracy but scale poorly
to large ontologies (>100K entities). To achieve the top performance, the approaches often sacriice recall for
eiciency by iltering some entities. Future research should explore strategies that allow for eicient embedding
generation without sacriicing performance by applying better space reduction techniques that ilter entities
with low similarity probability.
These limitations aren’t isolated technical challenges but interconnected symptoms of a deeper issue: embedding approaches have prioritized relational representation capacity over similarity-aware embeddings. Future
breakthroughs will come from embeddings explicitly optimized for the alignment task’s unique requirements for
semantic equivalence detection under heterogeneity, with minimal supervision, and maximal interpretability.

7 Future Research Directions
The study of entity-level segmentation and information-level aggregation shows that embedding models for
ontology matching beneit from aggregating as much information as possible. Given the typical structure of
ontology graphs, comprising nodes with textual features and logical properties, a model capable of representing
this information would likely combine language models and GNNs to embed both speciic properties and logical
constraints. However, jointly training these two model types is computationally expensive, as high-performing
language models typically comprise a large number of layers that incur signiicant processing overhead, while
graph neural networks must operate over large-scale graphs containing millions of triples. Therefore, more
eicient methods for training these models in tandem need to be developed to enable the computation of eicient
data batches and gradient updates, driving the learning of parameters for both GNNs and language models.

With the right combination of these approaches and optimal lexical segmentation, a matcher can achieve better
representation capacity, generalize more efectively across domains, and accumulate knowledge, ultimately
resulting in improved alignment performance.
Even when a suitable model exists for capturing the features necessary to generate embeddings for similarity
tasks, such as identifying equivalent entities in ontology matching, the availability of adequate training data
remains a critical requirement. Neural models generally require large volumes of data to learn their objectives
efectively, and in the context of ontology matching, such data takes the form of reference alignments between
ontologies. These alignments are typically produced either manually by domain experts or with the assistance of
automatic matching systems, both of which render the data generation process time-consuming and resourceintensive. Moreover, matching systems must not only identify correct correspondences but also reliably reject
incorrect ones, resulting in a highly imbalanced training set in which true correspondences constitute only a small
fraction of all possible entity pairs. This issue becomes even more pronounced in complex matching scenarios,
where the number of candidate subgraph combinations grows exponentially. In this context, the construction
of large-scale, openly available alignment datasets would substantially beneit supervised models by providing
the breadth of examples needed to learn robust similarity patterns and, consequently, produce more accurate
embeddings. As a complementary direction, the reinement of unsupervised techniques that rely on pretext tasks
represents a viable alternative for reducing data dependency while maintaining competitive performance in
ontology matching.
Recent advancements in NLP have led to the development of LLMs, which possess billions of parameters
and demonstrate signiicant abilities in language comprehension and generation. Given their success in various
semantic tasks, LLMs have the potential to enhance performance in Ontology Matching tasks. For instance, He et
al. [45] apply LLMs in conjunction with BERTMap, prompting the model to assess the semantic equivalence of
entity pairs and using its responses to establish the inal correspondences. Another work [93] leverages LLMs by
retrieving information from ontologies and constructing prompts to assess entity similarities. Similarly, OLaLa
[47] uses LLMs to compute core similarity metrics between entities, with diferent prompt sets tested to evaluate
their impact on performance. In [85], the authors propose using ChatGPT [86] for ontology alignment by querying
the language model with a template to assess the similarity of ontology entities. Those trends demonstrate that
LLMs will be even more present in the scenario of ontology matching in future works.
Another promising direction for future research lies in the further investigation of the underexplored areas
identiied by the proposed classiication scheme. In particular, the development of more robust techniques
for encoding relational properties within embeddings, as well as the systematic integration of external data
sources, represent valuable yet insuiciently addressed capabilities in current matching systems. With respect
to alignment complexity, complex ontology matching remains a largely underexplored frontier, especially
concerning the application of embedding-based methods. Future research in this area should prioritize three
interconnected challenges: the construction of dedicated benchmark datasets, the design of principled methods
for subgraph selection, and the advancement of techniques for embedding aggregation in the context of complex
correspondences.

8 Conclusion
This survey presents a comprehensive analysis of the role of embedding-based approaches in ontology matching.
The methodology is described in detail, encompassing the paper selection criteria, the databases queried, and
the manual post-iltering process applied, resulting in a curated corpus of relevant works. Building upon a prior
classiication scheme, the framework was extended by introducing a new dimension distinguishing between
simple and complex matching. This addition enables a more nuanced categorization of existing approaches,
facilitating the grouping of works according to how embedding techniques are employed within their respective

matching processes. The analysis uncovers a set of ongoing challenges and limitations that must be addressed in
order to advance matcher performance. Notably, while entity-level segmentation holds potential for improving
cross-domain generalization, the number of works tackling complex matching tasks remains limited, representing
a signiicant gap in the current literature.
Several limitations of existing works were identiied, and directions for future research on the use of embeddings
in ontology matching were outlined. One promising avenue is the integration of language models with GNNs,
which would allow systems to jointly leverage textual data and relational information encoded in ontology
graphs. Another critical direction concerns the development of large, openly available alignment datasets, which
would enable supervised models to more efectively learn the semantic representations required for ontology
entity matching. Taken together, these indings underscore the utility of the proposed classiication scheme
as a guiding framework for the analysis of embedding-based approaches in ontology matching. By organizing
existing systems along well-deined dimensions, the framework allows researchers to more readily compare
methodological choices and identify opportunities for the development of more robust and efective matching
techniques.
In conclusion, the indings presented in this survey highlight the importance of embedding-based approaches
in ontology matching and emphasize the need for further research in this area. Several directions of future
work include: the development of contextual and explainable embeddings that coherently integrate semantic,
structural, and justiication mechanisms; the advancement of cross-lingual and cross-domain embeddings capable
of supporting more diverse and heterogeneous ontology matching scenarios; the design of interactive, human-inthe-loop systems that systematically incorporate expert feedback into the embedding reinement process; and the
construction of scalable models able to handle large-scale ontologies eiciently without compromising matching
accuracy.

A.2 Analysis of the works using embeddings that participated in OAEI
The results of the matchers using embeddings that participated in the OAEI campaign from 2021 to 2024 are
present in Figure 10. Among the matchers that participated, KGMatcher was one of the most successful approaches
considering all tracks in 2021. It has a high F-measure in the tracks BioDiv, LargeBio, and Phenotype. Considering
its classiication in this survey, it has subword lexical unit segmentation, and it is pre-trained and trained with
data for each ontology. Despite its performance, KGMatcher uses no-context embeddings without the use of
background knowledge. ALOD2Vec is the second one, having a high F-measure in the tracks KnowledgeGraph
and LargeBio. ALOD2Vec uses entity-level segmentation and is pre-trained only, not requiring extra training. It
also includes background knowledge. Other notable systems include OLaLa, which performed well in the BioDiv
track in 2023 and applies subword segmentation with pre-training only. In the Anatomy track, recent systems
like SEBMatcher (2022), MDMapper (2024), and SORBETMatcher (2023) also show competitive results. These
methods typically use subword segmentation and both training and pre-training, though they do not leverage
background knowledge. In the Conference track, GraphMatcher achieved high performance in both 2023 and
2021. It uses subword segmentation, is trained and pre-trained, and relies on reference alignment, but does not
incorporate background knowledge. For the LargeBio track, OTMapOnto (2021) stands out with a combination of
word and subword segmentation, pre-training, and the use of background knowledge. Lastly, CIDER-LM, the
top performer in MultiFarm 2022, also uses subword segmentation, is trained and pre-trained, and is based on
reference alignments, though it does not include relational reasoning or background knowledge.
